# Carrier engine queue overflow

The four AWG carrier modes in the [prior discussion](../zero-loss-delay/summary.md) are `socks5-awg-{ext,req}-{tcp,utp}`. They are distinct from the TLS-only transport modes. Extended peers may negotiate the existing TLS-protected record layer around AWG datagrams; the lab leaves that production negotiation unchanged.

The hub splits outbound TUN GSO frames into individual IP packets and enqueues them into a 256-packet queue per mode. The AWG reader currently dequeues one carrier packet per call, then can block feeding its bounded peer or encryption queue. On the output side Extended builds a record and writes to a reliable stream, while Request/piece consumes a peer-issued request credit per piece and waits when none is ready. A µTP `Write` waits when its send/congestion or remote receive window is full. None of these limits is a `netem` drop. An overloaded carrier can therefore produce hub drops even with a lossless underlay.

## Matched experiments

Linux real TUN and stock endpoint offloads, no configured loss or rate ceiling, eight CUBIC download streams, 2 s omitted and 5 s nominal in the initial screen and its follow-ups, 0 and +200 ms added RTT. Client identical across arms. These are transparent TUN tests, not SOCKS CONNECT. Compare speed and p95 along with drops: an apparently cleaner hub counter can hide downstream queueing. `drops_per_hub_TUN_pct` uses hub TUN qdisc packet counts as the denominator; it is not end-to-end loss.

- **Four or eight ready packets on every read:** Extended/TCP at +200 ms went from 5,349 drops and 313 ms p95 (median of the two controls) to 779 drops and 533 ms p95 at eight packets. Request/µTP at +200 ms with four packets reached 6,592 drops and 455 ms p95, versus 1,027–7,521 drops and 307–308 ms p95 in controls. Not a safe common policy.
- **Four packets only when the engine queue is at least half full:** the follow-up restricted this to Extended/µTP and Request/TCP and repeated A/B/B/A. Extended/µTP at +200 ms delivered 199 → 233 Mbit/s, 8,483 → 1,842 drops, p95 315 → 310 ms. At 0 ms it delivered 363 → 388 Mbit/s but had 247 → 781 drops and p95 37.5 → 44.0 ms. Request/TCP's WAN result did not separate from control scatter; local medians were 513 → 493 Mbit/s and p95 23.2 → 28.7 ms. Neither is a common improvement across the two profiles.
- **Reuse the per-connection buffer for default varied Request/piece framing:** isolated sender benchmark reduced 1 allocation (1,792 bytes) to 0 per frame. This did not establish fewer engine drops in real TUN: local Request/TCP dropped 4,190 versus 1,000–2,303 across baseline runs. Allocation overhead is real, but removing it alone does not resolve the queue problem.
- **Limit unsent outer TCP data to 32 KiB, on top of eight-packet engine reads:** at +200 ms Request/TCP dropped 3,089 → 1,127, but throughput fell 171 → 142 Mbit/s and p95 rose 339 → 400 ms. Extended/TCP saw more hub drops and variable latency. This experiment changes only the socket option on the batching build, not the system TCP defaults.
- **Cap hub TUN GSO bursts at eight segments:** same binaries in all arms, four-second measurements, A/B/A. Request/µTP at +200 ms improved from 156 to 178 Mbit/s, 6,114 to 4,028 drops and 313 to 309 ms p95. Extended/µTP in the same series regressed from 4,357 to 5,037 drops and from 230 to 254 ms p95. The link attribute is shared across modes and native tunnels; this was only a namespace experiment, not a production setting.

The synthetic regression probe reproduces two rejected packets after a full engine queue is drained by just one read, and confirms the four-packet candidate can admit the same burst. It does not predict CPU scheduling, carrier backpressure, or p95 under concurrent network traffic. An isolated baseline Request/TCP trial also stalled while establishing the eighth data stream, before any data was transferred; the queue counters for that attempt did not move. This is not evidence of the overflow mechanism.

**Decision:** no tested candidate is suitable as the default across the two profiles. Keep the existing production path unchanged. Extended/µTP is the strongest candidate for path-aware batching: it cut WAN drops by 78% in the paired run, but made the local queue noisier. Before revisiting it, measure wait time at the engine queue, request-credit wait and blocked stream writes separately; the current drop counter identifies where packets are rejected, not which downstream stage is limiting each run.
